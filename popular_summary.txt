Societies organize themselves into complementary roles -- leader and follower,
planner and executor -- without anyone assigning them. How does such structure
arise from individual choices, and can it be seen coming?

We show that role formation behaves like an engine that runs on information.
Individuals read a noisy social signal to judge who should take which role, and
acting on that judgment sharpens the signal for everyone else. Past a threshold
the loop sustains itself and roles crystallize. Pushed further, roles appear in
a sequence whose shape the society sets for itself: sometimes gradual and
predictable, sometimes a sudden avalanche with no warning.

That sequence is already encoded in how individual behavior varies before any
role appears, so it can be read out in advance. This offers a way to anticipate
the spontaneous emergence of coordinated roles among interacting artificial
agents, while intervention is still possible.
